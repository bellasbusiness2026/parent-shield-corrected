# ParentShield P3 — Other-States Recording Consent Pack

**Scope:** Audio recording of conversations the **parent is a party to** (in-person or telephone), for all 50 states + DC.  
**Starting map (reputable secondary):** [Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press — Introduction to the Reporter’s Recording Guide](https://www.rcfp.org/introduction-to-reporters-recording-guide/) (updated Oct. 2025 per RCFP). Cross-checked against [Recording Law — US Recording Laws by State (2026 matrix)](https://www.recordinglaw.com/united-states-recording-laws/) and selected official statute pages.  
**Verified window:** 2026-09-21 (America/Chicago).

## Critical flags (read first)

1. **Interstate calls:** When participants are in different states, courts may apply the **stricter** state’s rule. Err toward all-party notice if any party is in an all-party / stricter jurisdiction. See RCFP intro (interstate phone calls).
2. **Hidden cameras / video:** Separate statutes often apply (especially where audio is captured, or in bathrooms / changing areas / private places). This table is **not** a hidden-camera guide.
3. **CPS / DFPS-specific rules:** Agency interview policies, Family Code notice statutes (e.g., Texas Fam. Code §§ 261.307 / 261.3027), and court orders can impose duties **beyond** general wiretap consent. Do not assume general consent law fully answers a CPS interaction.
4. **Not legal advice.** Classifications are simplified. Expectation-of-privacy, “confidential communication,” criminal-purpose exceptions, and civil remedies vary. Confirm current statute text and consult counsel.

### Classification key

| consent_type | Meaning in this pack |
| --- | --- |
| `one-party` | Generally, a participant may record without notifying other parties (subject to privacy / unlawful-purpose limits). |
| `all-party` | Generally, all parties to a private/confidential communication must consent (sometimes called “two-party”). |
| `mixed/unclear` | Rule depends on medium (phone vs in-person), place, judicial gloss, or reputable sources disagree / statute is ambiguous. |

### RCFP starting-map snapshot (secondary)

- **Primarily all-party (~11):** California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan *(RCFP notes third-party emphasis; participant recording is litigated)*, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Washington.
- **Split by medium:** Missouri & Oregon (stricter for many **in-person** settings; phone often one-party); Connecticut & Nevada (stricter for many **phone** settings; in-person often one-party).
- **Private-place overlays:** Hawaii & Maine (all-party features in particularly private places; otherwise often one-party).
- **Vermont:** No comprehensive state recording statute comparable to others; **federal** one-party baseline (18 U.S.C. § 2511) is commonly treated as controlling — still `one-party` here with that caveat.

Where Recording Law (2026) and RCFP diverge (notably **Delaware**, **Michigan**, **Nevada**, **Missouri**), this pack uses `mixed/unclear` or an explicit caveat rather than forcing a false binary.

## High-uncertainty / soften-first jurisdictions

Flag these for counsel review before product copy goes live:

| Jurisdiction | Why flagged |
| --- | --- |
| Delaware | All-party statutory flavor vs participant-recording ambiguity across secondaries. |
| Michigan | All-party-looking statute + participant exception case law. |
| Nevada | Phone vs in-person split (*Lane* / NRS 200.620 vs 200.650). |
| Missouri | Phone one-party vs cautious in-person private-conversation treatment. |
| Oregon | In-person notice rule vs phone one-party; recent federal litigation history. |
| Connecticut | Civil all-party phone rule vs criminal in-person participant framing. |
| Hawaii / Maine | Private-place overlays. |
| Vermont | Relies heavily on federal baseline. |
| Mississippi / Alaska | Secondary cite numbering less stable across surveys—confirm official code. |
